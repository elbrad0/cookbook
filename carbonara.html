Carbonara

Smoked streaky bacon, yolks only, Pecorino and Parmigiano. Every heat step comes with what to look and listen for, because "medium" means something different on every hob.

1 serving ~25 min
The one thing that matters most. Egg yolk starts to set at roughly 65–70 °C, so the pan has to be cooled before the egg goes in. A heavy pan (stainless steel or cast iron) holds a lot of heat, so it cools slowly. Step 7 cools it with a splash of pasta water, not by waiting. If in doubt, finish in a bowl.

Ingredients

75 gSpaghetti
3 rashers
(~60 g)		Smoked streaky bacon, cut into lardons about 1 cm wide
2Egg yolksFridge-cold is fine. Keep the whites for something else.
12 g + 13 gPecorino Romano + Parmigiano Reggiano, finely grated (25 g total), plus a little extra to finishHalf and half is the default. All Pecorino is the Roman way: saltier and sharper.
2 g
(~¾ tsp)		Black pepper, freshly ground, plus more to finish
10 g
(~1¾ tsp)		Salt, for the pasta waterLess than usual: the bacon and Pecorino both bring salt.
1.5 LWater, for the pasta
3 tbsp
(45 ml)		Water, for the bacon pan
1 tspSaved bacon fat Optional: fat poured off from an earlier fry. Helps against sticking and makes up for UK bacon's low fat yield.

Kit and heat

2.5–3 L pot with lid, on your largest burner
Heavy frying pan, stainless steel or cast iron, on a medium burner
Mug for reserved pasta water
Small bowl + fork for the egg mix
Tongs, or a sieve
Wooden or silicone spatula for the fond
A spare mixing bowl, warmed with hot tap water, as the fallback in step 8
Hobs vary a lot. Go by the cues in each step, not by the knob position.
Electric, ceramic or induction: the cues are the same. Electric and ceramic rings stay hot after you turn them off, so whenever a step says the burner goes off, lift the pan off the ring as well.

Method

  1. largest burner · high~8–10 min to boil

    1.5 L water in the pot, lid on. Don't salt it yet. Your biggest burner running hot is exactly what you want here.

    why this burner, why less water

    A big burner can be too fierce for frying, but boiling water can't overheat: it's capped at 100 °C, so the extra heat just gets you there faster. Less water than usual means the starch the pasta sheds is more concentrated, and that starch is what holds the sauce together in step 8.

  2. no heat
    • Cut the bacon into lardons, about 1 cm wide.
    • Separate the yolks into the small bowl.
    • Grate both cheeses finely and add them to the yolks with the pepper. Mix with a fork into a thick, stiff paste. It loosens in step 6.
    why grate finely

    Fine cheese melts into the egg in seconds. Coarse shreds take longer than the egg does to set, so they end up as stringy clumps.

  3. medium burner · lowest~10–15 min total

    Lardons into the cold pan, with the 3 tbsp water (and the saved fat, if using). Spread them out in one layer. Then turn on the burner.

    It goes in two phases. First the water bubbles and the fat slowly turns translucent. When the water has gone, the sound changes from bubbling to sizzling, and now you're frying in rendered fat. Stir every minute or so.

    RightSoft bubbling, then a steady light sizzle, like light rain. The fat goes from white to translucent to golden. Fond on the base stays amber to mid-brown.
    Too hotA loud sizzle straight away, or the lean browning while the fat is still white and rubbery, or the fond darkening towards coffee, or a sharp smell. Lift the pan off for 20–30 seconds and add a splash of water.
    Too coolStill silent, fat still white, after about 5 minutes. Nudge the heat up a little.

    Milky white foam early on is normal. That's brine from the cure, not a failure. Don't turn the heat up to chase it off.

    If lardons stick: a small splash of water at the edge of the pan, then scrape. It dissolves the sugary brine that glues them down.

    why the water and the cold start

    Water can't get hotter than 100 °C, so while it's in the pan it holds the bacon at a temperature where fat melts out but the meat doesn't brown fast. That gives the fat time to render before the lean dries out. The sound change is your signal that the cap has gone. Wet-cured UK bacon leaks brine with sugar in it. When the water boils off, that dries into a glue under the meat, which is why it sticks and why water releases it. Stirring lardons stops the glue setting in one place.

  4. largest burner · highpacket time minus 1 min

    When the bacon has switched from bubbling to sizzling, salt the boiling water and add the spaghetti.

    The strands will stick out of the pot at first. After 30–60 seconds the part under the water softens. Push the ends down with a spoon or tongs so the strands bend round the pot, then stir so they don't stick together. Stir again a couple of times in the first two minutes.

    RightA rolling boil: big bubbles that keep going when you stir. Taste the water: noticeably salty, but less so than the sea.
    Boiling overStarchy foam rising to the rim. Lid off and turn down a touch. It only needs to keep boiling.
    why this timing

    The bacon takes longer than the pasta, and it's happy to wait off the heat. The pasta isn't. Starting the pasta once the bacon is sizzling means the bacon finishes first and the pasta is never kept waiting.

  5. bacon burner · off

    Turn the bacon burner off when it's done. Leave the bacon and its fat in the pan.

    DoneFat golden and translucent with crisp edges, lean still slightly chewy, not brittle. Foam down to a few small bubbles. A visible layer of clear fat in the pan. Fond amber to mid-brown.
    Too farFond turning coffee-dark or black, or bacon gone hard and dry. Off now. Dark fond will make the sauce bitter, so leave the darkest patches behind in step 7.
  6. no heat

    About a minute before the pasta's done, scoop a mugful of pasta water. Whisk 1 tablespoon of it into the egg-and-cheese paste, a little at a time. It should go from stiff paste to a thick, pourable cream.

    why

    A thinner sauce spreads over the pasta in a second or two, so no part of it sits on a hot spot long enough to scramble. The hot water also starts melting the cheese, and the starch in it helps the fat and egg stay emulsified instead of splitting.

  7. both burners off

    Turn the pasta burner off. Pour about 3 tablespoons of the reserved pasta water into the bacon pan. It will hiss and bubble. Scrape up the fond with the spatula as it does.

    Then lift the pasta straight in with tongs (or drain through a sieve) and toss for about 30 seconds to coat it in the fat.

    ReadyThe pan has stopped sizzling. There's a little starchy liquid in the base, and steam is wisping off, not billowing.
    Too hotStill spitting or sizzling where the pasta touches the metal. Add another tablespoon of pasta water and toss. If it's still sizzling after that, tip everything into the warmed bowl and do step 8 there.
    why water, not waiting

    A heavy pan holds much more heat than a thin one, so it cools slowly. Turning water into steam soaks up a lot of heat, so the splash pulls the pan down quickly and stops at about 100 °C while the water lasts. The pasta then takes away more. Same trick as the bacon start, used in reverse. It also dissolves the fond, and the brine glue in it, into the sauce, so that flavour isn't wasted.

  8. off the heat~1 min

    Pour the egg mix over the pasta and toss and stir vigorously, straight away. Add pasta water a tablespoon at a time until the sauce is right.

    RightGlossy, pale gold, coats every strand and flows slowly. It thickens a little as you toss.
    Too hotGrainy texture, small yellow curds, egg setting on the pan base. Tip into the warmed bowl immediately, add a tablespoon of pasta water, and toss hard. Small curds will smooth out.
    Too coolThin and runny with a raw-egg sheen, and not thickening after a minute of tossing. Back on the bacon burner at its lowest for 10 seconds while tossing, then off. Repeat until it thickens.
    why keep moving

    The sauce is an emulsion of fat and water held together by the egg and the pasta starch. Heat thickens the yolk proteins into a sauce, but too much heat sets them into curds. Constant tossing spreads the heat evenly, so the whole sauce thickens at once instead of one patch overcooking.

  9. no heat

    Plate straight away. Finish with a little more cheese and a few grinds of pepper. It tightens as it cools, so eat it now.

Rescue

Scrambled

Into a warm bowl, a tablespoon of pasta water, toss hard. Small curds smooth out. Big ones won't, but it'll still taste good.

Too thick or claggy

More pasta water, a tablespoon at a time, tossing between each.

Too thin

Ten-second bursts on the lowest heat, tossing the whole time. Or a little more grated cheese.

Bacon stuck to the pan

A small splash of water at the edge, then scrape. It's the cure's sugary brine; water dissolves it.

Notes

  • Yolks only gives a richer, more golden sauce than whole eggs.
  • The yolks are only gently cooked. British Lion-stamped eggs are considered safe eaten runny for most people.
  • Dry-cured streaky bacon, if you find it, gives more fat and less brine, so less sticking.
  • Saved bacon fat keeps for weeks in the fridge as long as it was poured off clean, without fond in it.
