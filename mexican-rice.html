Mexican Rice with Black Beans

Rice toasted in butter with fried spices, simmered in stock, with black beans steamed on top. Every heat step comes with what to look and listen for, because "medium" means something different on every hob.

Kit and heat

  • A wide, heavy frying or sauté pan, about 24–28 cm, with a lid that fits. Wide means far more grains touch the metal when you toast them. A heavy base spreads the heat, so the bottom layer of rice doesn't catch. No matching lid? The lid of a large pot often fits.
  • A sieve, a jug, a wooden spoon, a fork, a timer and a scale. A small 0.1 g digital scale is ideal for the spices, but measuring spoons work too; both are given below.
  • A heavy pan is slow to respond. All that metal stores heat, so turning the heat down takes a minute or more to show. If anything looks too hot, lift the pan off the heat for 20–30 seconds instead of relying on the knob.
  • Hobs vary a lot. On some, the lowest setting of the biggest burner is still very hot. Go by the cues in each step, not by the knob position.
  • Burners: fry, toast and boil on a medium burner. Simmer on your smallest burner, on its lowest setting. A small flame under a wide, heavy pan works well: the thick base carries the heat sideways, and gentle heat is what absorption rice needs.
  • Electric, ceramic or induction: the cues are the same. Electric and ceramic rings are slow to cool when you turn them down. For the simmer, set a second ring to its lowest a few minutes ahead and move the pan across, rather than waiting for a hot ring to cool.

Chillies: the job is split in two. The large reds are mild and give flesh and fruitiness; the finger chilli gives the heat. A jalapeño does both jobs in one, so 1 jalapeño can stand in for all three, though this version hasn't been cooked with one.

Pepper: yellow and orange peppers are ripe, so they're sweeter than green. Expect a rounder, sweeter rice. The lime balances it.

Oil: the butter gives the flavour; the oil lifts the temperature the butter can take before its milk solids brown.

Easy-cook rice only. The liquid and times are worked out for parboiled rice. Ordinary long-grain absorbs less and cooks faster, so these amounts won't transfer.

Prep

  1. Why drain so long: wet rice can't toast. The pan has to boil off every drop of rinse water before the grains get hotter than 100 °C, so surface water means steaming instead of toasting.

  2. SharpHeat straight away: scrape the membrane out before chopping.
    SlowHeat builds after a second or two: leave the membrane in.

    Why the membrane: most of a chilli's heat (capsaicin) sits in the white membrane, not the flesh. Wash your hands afterwards and keep them away from your eyes.

  6. RightIt tastes like a well-seasoned soup. No more salt needed.
    BlandAdd a pinch of salt and stir, then taste again.

    Why season the jug: the rice absorbs all of this liquid, so however salty the stock is, the rice will be. Salt added at the end mostly sits on the surface of the grains.

Cooking

  1. Medium burner · low end; set by the cue
    ReadyThe butter foams, then the foam starts to settle. A piece of onion dropped in sizzles gently.
    Not yetThe butter has melted but sits quiet and flat. Wait.
    Too hotThe butter turns brown or smokes within a minute. Lift the pan off for 20–30 s.
  2. Same burner, same heat
    RightA soft fizz, small bubbles round the pieces. The onion turns translucent and the pepper goes soft.
    Too hotThe onion edges brown in the first couple of minutes. Lift off, then lower the heat.
    Too coolNo sound at all.

    Why the salt: it draws water out of the onion so it softens faster, and seasons the veg itself. Keep it to a pinch: in absorption rice nothing leaves the pan, so every bit of salt ends up in the rice, and the stock brings plenty.

    Why the chilli goes in now: capsaicin dissolves in fat, so frying it here spreads the heat evenly through every grain. You won't get hot spots.

  3. RightA strong garlic smell, still pale.
    Too hotThe garlic goes golden or brown. It turns bitter fast, so lift the pan off.
  4. RightWithin 30 s the spice smell blooms. The purée darkens from bright red to brick or rust. A little catching on the base is fine; the stock lifts it.
    Too hotBlack patches, or a sharp, acrid smell. Lift off at once.

    Why fry them: the flavour compounds in cumin and paprika dissolve in fat, not water, so a short fry releases far more than stirring them into the stock would. Frying the purée cooks out its raw, tinny taste. That's the colour change.

  5. Same burner, same heat
    RightSmell first: nutty, like popcorn. The grains slide freely and you hear a light, dry ticking. Colour is a weak cue with this rice: parboiled grains start pale amber, and only deepen a little.
    Still wetSteam rising, grains clumped, no ticking. The rinse water is still cooking off. Keep stirring; it can take an extra minute.
    Too hotBrown spots within a minute, or a sharp smell. Lift off for 20–30 s, keep stirring.

    Why toast: it firms the starch on the outside of each grain and starts browning reactions even where you can't see colour. The grains stay separate and pick up a nutty flavour. The wide pan helps: far more grains touch the metal at once.

  6. Medium burner to boil → smallest burner · lowest

    Why not stir: stirring rubs starch off the grains and breaks them, which makes rice gluey. It also flattens the little steam channels that form through the rice and carry heat up to the top layer.

  7. Smallest burner · lowest
    RightA soft, steady hiss or gentle bubbling. A little steam at the lid's edge.
    LivelyThe lid rattles, steam jets out, or liquid spits at the rim. Leave it running to 12 min, then turn the heat off for everything after; the pan's stored heat finishes the job.
    Too coolSilence and no steam at all after 3 min. Nudge the heat up a touch.

    Why gentle matters: by the end, the rice has absorbed almost all the liquid, and the bottom layer of grains sits directly on hot metal.

  8. RightNo liquid pooling when tilted. Small holes across the surface where steam has been venting. Top grains nearly tender.
    Too wetLiquid pools at the low side. Lid on, 3 more min, check again.
    Too dryA dry crackle from the base, or no liquid and the top grains still chalky. Drizzle 2–3 tbsp boiling water over, lid on, 3 more min.
  9. Smallest burner · lowest, or off if it was lively

    Why on top: they warm through in the steam and pick up its flavour, without getting crushed or stealing liquid from the rice.

  10. Heat off

    Why rest: steam keeps moving from the wetter top down through to the drier base, so moisture evens out top to bottom and the grains firm up. Lift the lid early and that steam escapes, leaving the top wet and the bottom underdone.

  11. DoneBite a grain from the middle: tender all the way through, grains separate. A thin golden layer stuck to the base is fine and tastes good.
    ChalkyA hard white centre. Drizzle 2 tbsp boiling water over, lid on, 5 min on the lowest heat.
    WetSoft and clumping. Spread it out, lid off, lowest heat for 2 min.
    BurntA burnt smell from the base. Don't scrape the base in; serve from the top.

    Why fork and lime last: a spoon presses grains together; a fork lifts them apart. Acid lifts the rich, buttery base, but its fresh taste fades with heat, so it goes in off the heat.
