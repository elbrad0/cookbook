Fluffy Scrambled Eggs

Steak was the first thing I learned to cook, so when I got round to scrambled eggs I cooked them the same way: hot pan, fast. Along the way I picked up a couple of tricks, a better whisk technique and getting the butter sizzling before the eggs go in. Big fluffy curds, rich with butter, finished with a decent hot sauce.

1 serving ~5 min
The one thing that matters most. This cooks in a couple of minutes, so have everything within reach before the egg goes in. Getting everything ready before you start helps me, but this one's quick enough that you can lay things out while the pan heats. Judge the heat by the butter, not the dial: it should sizzle the moment it touches the pan.

Ingredients

2–3Eggs
~20 gButter, a generous knobIt's hard to overdo. Too little is the usual mistake; extra makes little difference except more flavour.
to tasteSalt and black pepper, at the table
generousHot sauceCholula Original or Cholula Chipotle. Original is quite salty, which suits eggs, so taste before adding salt. Skip the Green Pepper one here; it works better with chicken.

Kit and heat

Small non-stick pan, about 20 cm
Two spatulas, or a spatula and a wooden spoon
Measuring jug or mixing bowl + fork for whisking
Plate, knife and fork, laid out before you start
Why two tools. One works, but this cooks fast, and a second tool lets you turn and break up the curds twice as quickly.
On gas: keep the flame inside the base of the pan. A small pan on a big burner wastes heat up the sides and scorches the handle.
Electric, ceramic or induction: the cues are the same. Go by the butter in step 3, not the setting.

Method

  1. small pan · high~1–2 min

    Pan on high. While it heats:

    • Rest the knob of butter on one of the spatulas, ready to tip in.
    • Lay out the plate, knife and fork, salt, pepper and hot sauce.
    ReadyThe air just above the pan looks wavy, and a flick of water sizzles straight off.
    Too hotAny smoke or sharp smell from the empty pan. Lift it off the heat for 30 seconds.
    why not leave it heating

    Non-stick coatings start to break down at around 260 °C, and an empty pan on high gets there fast because there's nothing in it to absorb the heat. Hot is the goal; smoking is past it.

  2. no heat~30–45 sec

    Crack the eggs into the jug or bowl. Poke the yolks a few times with the fork to break them.

    Tilt the container over until it's nearly on its side. The whole motion is a circle from the wrist, with your arm staying still. Move the fork down through the egg, then up and over the surface, so the egg folds over itself and traps air underneath. You don't need to go fast, though it helps.

    RightOne even yellow colour, no streaks of white, and large bubbles sitting on top when you stop.
    Not yetStreaks of clear white, or only small bubbles. Keep folding.
    why fold, and why an even colour

    Lifting the fork up and over uses the egg's surface tension to wrap a sheet of egg around a pocket of air, which beats stirring round and round. An even colour matters too: streaks of white set before the rest and turn rubbery.

  3. small pan · high~20–30 sec

    Tip the butter into the pan. While it melts, give the eggs a second whisk the same way.

    RightThe butter sizzles the moment it touches the pan, foams, and starts to smell slightly nutty.
    Too hotThe butter turns brown within seconds, or smokes. Lift the pan off briefly; the eggs can still go in.
    Too coolThe butter just sits and melts quietly with no sizzle. Wait for the pan to catch up before the eggs go in.
    what the butter is telling you

    Butter is about 15% water. The sizzle and foam are that water boiling off, which tells you the pan is well above 100 °C. Once it's gone, the milk solids start to toast, which is the nutty smell.

  4. small pan · high~1–2 min

    When the butter is about 80–90% melted, pour the eggs in. Keep them moving with both tools. They'll try to set into a rough omelette; break that up into big curds as you go.

    RightThe edges set within seconds and the eggs puff up into big, soft curds.
    Too coolThe egg spreads out thin and sits wet without setting. Leave the heat on high and keep moving.
    why fast and hot

    Two things puff the curds up: the air you folded in, and the egg's own water flashing to steam in the hot pan. Cooking fast sets the egg around both before they can escape, which is where the fluffy texture comes from. On low heat the air leaks out before anything sets, and you get small, dense curds.

  5. off the heat

    I cook mine well done because of texture, but cook yours to taste. Either way, plate them a little before they reach the point you want, because they keep cooking in their own heat and on the plate.

    Well doneThe first little flecks of golden browning appear on some of the curds. Start plating now.
    SofterPlate before any browning shows, while the curds still look glossy.
    why the golden flecks

    Browning only starts once the water at a spot has boiled off, which lets that bit of egg get well above 100 °C. So the flecks mean the surface is drying out. Their colour comes from the egg browning and from the butter's milk solids toasting onto the curds, which is why plenty of butter brings them on.

  6. no heat

    Salt, pepper and a generous amount of hot sauce. If you're using Cholula Original, add the hot sauce first and taste before salting.

Rescue

Flat and dense

The pan wasn't hot enough, or the eggs needed more folding. Next time wait for the butter to sizzle on contact, and whisk until the bubbles on top are large.

Browning before they've set

The pan's too hot for the amount of egg. Lift it off the heat for a few seconds and keep the curds moving.

Sticking

Not enough butter, or a worn pan. Use the full knob; it's part of what keeps the egg from sticking.

Notes

  • This is a non-stick job. Cast iron and stainless steel work but grip egg much more, especially if the cast iron isn't well seasoned.
  • 2 eggs cooks fastest; 3 still fits a 20 cm pan comfortably.
  • The same fold-and-cook-fast method makes omelettes puff up like a soufflé.
